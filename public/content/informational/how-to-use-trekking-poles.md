# How to Use and Adjust Trekking Poles

Adjust trekking poles so your elbow bends about 90 degrees when the tip is on the ground and your hand is through the strap from below, on flat ground. Lengthen them a few inches for descents and shorten them for climbs, plant the pole opposite your forward foot as you walk and let the strap carry the load on your wrist, not a tight grip. Used well, poles take stress off your knees, add stability on rough ground and help you maintain rhythm.

Many hikers carry trekking poles and use them poorly: too long, wrist strap misused, tips dragging, locks slipping. Good technique makes the poles an effective tool that reduces fatigue on long days and heavy loads. This guide explains how to set the length, how to use the straps and grips, techniques for flat ground, climbs, descents and stream crossings, how locks and materials work, how poles double as tent supports, how to care for them and the mistakes that make poles a nuisance, not a help.

## Why use trekking poles

Poles do several things. They share the work of carrying you and your pack between your legs and arms, which reduces the force on your knees, especially on descents. Studies of walking mechanics have shown measurable reductions in knee load on steep downhills when poles are used, though the size varies by person and terrain. They improve balance on rocky, muddy or snowy ground and on stream crossings. They help set a rhythm on long climbs, and they push cobwebs and brush out of the way. Some ultralight tents use poles as the shelter's support, which saves weight; see the [best ultralight trekking pole tents](/tents-shelter/best-ultralight-trekking-pole-tents) list.

They also have costs. Poles take your hands, which makes it harder to use a camera, snack or map, and they add weight and a small amount of effort in the arms. For short, easy hikes on smooth trails, many people skip them. For long days, heavy packs, steep terrain and knee problems, poles are valuable.

## Setting the length

Start with the standard method for flat ground.

1. Stand upright with your arms relaxed at your sides.
2. Hold the pole with the tip on the ground, hand through the strap from below, and grip the handle.
3. Adjust the pole until your elbow is bent at about 90 degrees, with the forearm parallel to the ground.

Fine-tune for terrain:

- **Uphill:** Shorten the poles by 5 to 10 centimeters (2 to 4 inches), so your arms stay at a comfortable angle as the slope rises in front of you.
- **Downhill:** Lengthen them by the same amount, so you can plant the tips below you and take weight in your arms.
- **Traversing a slope:** Use the uphill pole shorter and the downhill pole longer, or use one pole at a time.

Many poles are labeled with height charts. A rough guide is that a person 5'6" uses a pole about 105 to 110 centimeters at the base setting, but use the elbow method rather than a chart.

## The wrist strap

The strap is meant to carry some of the load through your wrist, so your hand can stay relaxed. To use it correctly, put your hand up through the loop from underneath, then pull down and grip the handle so the strap lies between your hand and the grip. Adjust the strap so it is snug but not tight. When you push down, the strap supports the weight, and your fingers hold the pole lightly, avoiding the "death grip" that tires your forearms.

In some situations, such as avalanche terrain or steep, rocky scrambles where a fall is possible, you may prefer to keep your hand out of the strap so you can drop the pole if it snags. Know the risk. Some hikers choose straps for general hiking and remove them in hazardous places.

## Technique on flat and rolling ground

Walk naturally and let the poles swing in rhythm with your steps. Plant the pole opposite your forward foot, in the same pattern as your arms swing when you walk. The tip should touch down about even with or slightly behind your forward foot, and your arm should push down and back, not forward. A slight angle of the poles back from vertical gives good propulsion.

Do not drag the poles or plant them far ahead, which wastes energy and can cause a slip. Let your arms stay relaxed, with elbows near your sides. Keep your hands at about waist height. If you tire, you can carry the poles in one hand or stow them.

## Climbing

On climbs, shorten the poles and plant them slightly ahead of you, pushing down and back to help drive you up the slope. Use a rhythmic cadence, matching your breathing and steps. On steep sections, lean slightly forward and use short steps. Poles help take some load from your legs, particularly with a heavy pack.

If the grade is very steep and rocky, you may need your hands free, so stow the poles on your pack. Learn to collapse and stow them quickly, which makes transitions easier.

## Descending

Lengthen the poles and plant them ahead and below you, taking weight through your arms and straps as you step down. This reduces the force on knees and helps control speed. Keep your knees slightly bent and your weight over your feet. Use both poles together on steep drops, and place them where they will not slip.

On loose ground, test the plant before you commit your weight. Avoid putting a pole between rocks where it can wedge and snap. For long descents, check that the locks are tight, since a slipping pole can cause a fall.

## Stream crossings, snow and mud

Poles add stability on stream crossings, giving you three points of contact. Face upstream, plant the poles ahead and move one point at a time, keeping at least two points in contact. Undo your pack's hip belt and sternum strap before crossing, in case you need to shed the pack. Use poles to probe depth and footing. In mud and snow, use baskets, the small discs near the tip that stop poles from sinking, and consider longer poles for deep snow.

Water and drinking breaks matter during long days with poles. You may carry a filter or tablets so you can refill without long detours; see [water filter vs water purifier for camping](/packs-hiking/water-filter-vs-water-purifier), [how to use water purification tablets](/packs-hiking/how-to-use-water-purification-tablets) and how to use a filter straw to plan treatment, and stop to drink at safe spots so you do not slip near wet edges.

## Locks, materials and features

**Locks.** Poles adjust with one of two systems. Twist locks use an internal expander and are light but can slip if worn, and flip locks use a lever that clamps the sections and are easier to adjust with gloves and more reliable. Check that locks hold under load before every trip, and adjust the tension screw on lever locks.

**Materials.** Aluminum poles are cheaper and more durable, and they bend rather than snap. Carbon fiber poles are lighter and absorb vibration but can break under sharp impact. Choose by weight, budget and abuse.

**Grips.** Cork wicks sweat and molds to your hand, foam is soft and warm and rubber is durable and cheap. Extended grips below the handle let you choke up on steep sections without adjusting the pole.

**Tips and baskets.** Carbide tips grip rock, and rubber tip covers protect surfaces and are quieter. Many parks ask you to use rubber tips on delicate surfaces.

**Folding versus telescoping.** Folding poles pack small and suit travel and running, and telescoping poles are simpler and a bit sturdier.

Budget options exist: see the lists of the [best trekking poles under $50](/packs-hiking/best-trekking-poles-under-50), [under $75](/packs-hiking/best-trekking-poles-under-75) and [under $100](/packs-hiking/best-trekking-poles-under-100). Spend more for lighter weight and better locks if you use poles a lot.

## Packing poles

When you do not need poles, collapse them and strap them to the pack. Many packs have loops and straps for poles. Stow them with the tips pointing down and secured so they do not catch branches. If you carry them in a vehicle or an airplane, check the rules. A pole bag protects your gear and keeps tips from poking.

## Pole length chart and fine-tuning

The elbow method works for everyone, and a rough chart can give you a starting point before you try poles in person. Heights below assume a standard adjustable pole.

| Your height | Starting pole length (flat) | Uphill | Downhill |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5'0" (152 cm) | 95 to 100 cm | 90 to 95 cm | 100 to 105 cm |
| 5'4" (163 cm) | 100 to 105 cm | 95 to 100 cm | 105 to 110 cm |
| 5'8" (173 cm) | 105 to 110 cm | 100 to 105 cm | 110 to 115 cm |
| 6'0" (183 cm) | 110 to 115 cm | 105 to 110 cm | 115 to 120 cm |
| 6'4" (193 cm) | 115 to 120 cm | 110 to 115 cm | 120 to 125 cm |

These are approximations, and arm and torso proportions change the result. Check with the elbow test, and mark your preferred lengths on the pole with a strip of tape so you can return to them quickly. If you use a fixed-length pole or a folding pole, choose the size nearest your flat-ground length and adjust with the grip: choking up on climbs and holding the lower foam grip on traverses.

Many hikers find that their ideal length changes through the day as they tire or as the pack gets lighter. Check your posture every few hours: if your shoulders creep up, the poles are too long, and if you bend forward to reach the ground, they are too short.

## Poles with a heavy pack and for balance

When you carry a heavy load, poles take on more work. They help with balance on rough ground, reduce the strain on knees and ankles and give you extra stability on stream crossings and slippery slopes. With a heavy pack, take shorter steps, plant poles more deliberately and keep a slightly longer descent length. Poles also help if you have a wobbly ankle, a sore knee or a long-standing joint problem, and many people with arthritis find them very helpful, though anyone with a medical condition should discuss exercise with a clinician.

Poles can encourage better posture, but they can also encourage you to lean too heavily. Try to keep your torso upright and your steps natural. If you feel yourself hanging on the poles, shorten them and check your pace. The goal is a smooth rhythm that shares the load, not a way to hold you up.

## Using one pole versus two

Two poles are the default, giving symmetrical support and a steady rhythm. One pole is useful in specific situations: when you want a hand free for a camera, when you are hiking on a trail with a steep side, when you carry a child or when you are following a path with a narrow footing. A single pole on the uphill side of a traverse provides support and leaves your other hand free. Some ultralight hikers use one pole to save weight, and some use one pole and a camera in the other hand.

If you use a pole as a tent support, you need both, and some shelters use a single pole; check the specification of your tent. Always bring the poles you need for the shelter, and consider a spare tip or basket. When you use trekking poles to support a tent, the lengths are set to the tent's needs, not your walking posture, so measure and mark the setting.

## Poles in snow, sand and on rocky terrain

Snow requires large baskets that spread the load and stop poles from sinking. Summer baskets are small and designed for dirt and rocks, and they catch on roots and rocks if they are large. Change baskets to suit the conditions, and carry the winter ones if you expect snow. On sand, a basket helps keep the tip from sinking, and on slick rock, a rubber tip may slip, so use carbide tips on hard surfaces and rubber tips only where rules require them.

On rocky terrain, poles can get stuck in cracks. Plant them where the tip has a firm footing, and avoid wedging them between rocks, since leverage can snap a pole. On scree and loose slopes, keep the poles short to avoid a long lever that sinks and slips. If you need to use your hands for scrambling, stow the poles on the pack or in a pole holder, and do not carry them in your hands as you climb.

## Etiquette and trail impact

Poles can damage the trail and the plants when used carelessly. Use rubber tips where required or recommended, particularly on rock surfaces, in sensitive meadows and in busy places where sound carries. Plant poles on the trail, not in vegetation beside it. Do not use poles to knock on trees or poke at wildlife. In groups, watch where your poles swing and avoid hitting others. On narrow trails, keep poles close to your body when passing. Carry them securely on your pack when you are not using them so that they do not catch on branches or on other people. Small courtesies keep the trail pleasant for everyone.

## Buying notes

When you shop, think about weight, locks, grips and packed length. Heavier poles are more durable and cheaper, lighter poles reduce fatigue over a long day, and carbon poles help with vibration. Flip locks are easier to adjust than twist locks and handle cold and wet better. Cork grips suit sweaty hands, and foam grips suit cold weather. Packed length matters if you travel, so consider folding poles. Check the warranty, which often covers breakage, and replacement parts such as tips and baskets.

If you plan to use poles to pitch a tent, choose poles that reach the length required by your shelter and check compatibility. ## Care

Rinse poles after muddy, sandy or salty trips, and let them dry with the sections open. Clean the locks and sections to prevent corrosion and sticking. Check tips and baskets for wear and replace them. Do not store poles collapsed while wet. If a twist lock slips, clean it and replace the expander if needed. Periodically check the straps for wear and the grips for cracks. A well-kept pair of poles lasts for years.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Setting poles too long, which strains shoulders.
- Gripping the handle tightly and ignoring the strap.
- Planting poles ahead of the body and braking.
- Not adjusting length for climbs and descents.
- Using poles in terrain where hands are needed.
- Ignoring slipping locks.
- Leaving poles wet and collapsed.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should trekking poles be?
Adjust them so your elbow bends about 90 degrees on flat ground with the tip on the ground. Shorten for climbs and lengthen for descents.

### Do trekking poles really help knees?
They reduce the load on knees, especially on steep descents and with heavy packs, by transferring some of the force to your arms. The benefit varies by person and terrain.

### Should my hand go through the strap?
Yes in most hiking, from below, so the strap carries load and you can relax your grip. In terrain where a fall is possible, such as avalanche slopes, consider keeping your hand free.

### Are carbon poles better than aluminum?
Carbon is lighter and reduces vibration but can snap under impact. Aluminum is heavier, cheaper and tends to bend. Choose by weight, budget and how hard you use them.

### Can I use poles on flat trails?
Yes, though many people do not need them. They help with rhythm and posture, and they can reduce fatigue on long hikes. Use them as you like.

### How do I stop a pole from slipping?
Clean the lock, tighten the tension screw on a lever lock or replace a worn expander on a twist lock. Check before each trip, and replace a pole that will not hold.
