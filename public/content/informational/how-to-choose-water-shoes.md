# How to Choose Water Shoes

Choose water shoes by the surface you will walk on and the activity: a thin, flexible, quick-draining shoe with a grippy sole for beaches, pools and calm water, a thicker, protective sole with a toe guard for rocky shorelines and creeks, and a more structured sandal or hybrid shoe for hiking through water. The shoe should fit snugly, drain fast, dry fast and have a sole with enough traction to grip wet rock without being so stiff that it does not feel like a shoe.

Water shoes protect your feet from sharp rocks, shells, hot sand and slippery surfaces, and let you walk in and out of water without changing footwear. They differ from regular sneakers, which soak up water, take forever to dry and may feel like wearing sponges. This guide covers types of water shoes, soles, uppers, drainage, fit, use cases from swimming pools to river hiking, how they compare with sandals and hiking footwear, plus care and a quick buying checklist.

## Types of water shoes

**Minimalist aqua socks.** A thin neoprene or stretch fabric sock with a thin rubber sole. They are light, pack flat and give a barefoot feel. They are good for pools, sand and calm water, and poor for sharp rocks because the sole is thin.

**Slip-on water shoes.** A mesh upper with a molded rubber outsole and a drawcord or elastic closure. They are comfortable and easy to get on, and drain through the mesh. They fit beaches, boats, kayaking and casual use.

**Lace-up water shoes.** A more secure fit with laces, and often a thicker sole and toe cap. They suit walking on rocky beaches, creeks and trails.

**Water sandals.** Open-toed sandals with straps, a thick sole and sometimes a toe guard. They drain fast and are good for hot weather, river trips and rafting, though debris can enter, and the toes are less protected.

**Hybrid water hiking shoes.** Shoe-like uppers with drainage ports and rugged soles, built for walking on trails and through streams. They weigh more and cost more.

**Beach clogs and slides.** Casual and not suitable for water activities that need a secure fit.

## Soles: grip and protection

The sole is the most important part. Water shoe soles are made from rubber or a rubber-like compound, with patterns designed to grip wet surfaces. Look for a pattern with multi-directional lugs or sipes, tiny slits that channel water away and increase grip. A sticky rubber compound works better on wet rock, and a harder compound lasts longer.

Thickness matters for protection. A thin sole of 2 to 3 millimeters suits pools and sand but lets you feel every sharp stone. A thicker sole of 4 to 8 millimeters protects against rocks and shells and adds insulation from cold water or hot sand. A shoe with a rock plate or a stiffer midsole protects further but feels less flexible. Choose according to the surface: for a rocky beach or creek, thicker is better, and for swimming pool use, thin and flexible is fine. Some soles have an arch shape and a footbed that adds comfort for long wear.

## Uppers, drainage and drying

The upper is usually mesh, neoprene, knit or synthetic fabric. Mesh uppers drain quickly and breathe well. Neoprene retains a thin layer of warm water, which helps in cold water but dries slowly. Knit uppers are soft and flexible and conform to the foot. Look for drainage holes or ports at the sole and heel, which let water leave quickly, and for a quick-dry lining.

Drying time is a major factor in comfort and odor. A mesh shoe with open construction dries in an hour or two in sun and wind. Thick neoprene may take half a day. Wet shoes in a closed bag smell quickly, so dry them in the shade after use. Choose antimicrobial or odor-resistant treatments if you wear them often, and rinse them in fresh water after salt or chlorine.

## Fit and sizing

Water shoes should fit snugly, since your foot slides when wet, and a loose shoe can slip off in current. Your toes should have a little room, but no excess space. Choose a closure that adjusts: elastic laces, a drawcord with a toggle or a hook-and-loop strap hold the shoe on. Check the heel for security and for a pull tab that makes it easy to put on.

Size runs vary, so check the maker's chart. If you are between sizes, consider your socks, since some people wear thin neoprene socks with water shoes in cold water. Try them on in the afternoon, when feet are slightly swollen. Wide feet should look for a roomy toe box and stretchy upper, and narrow feet for a secure closure. The same principles that apply to boots, in [how hiking boots should fit](/clothing-footwear/how-hiking-boots-should-fit), apply in a lighter way to water shoes: toe room, heel hold and no pressure points.

**Swimming and pool exercise.** Thin, flexible shoes with a grippy sole prevent slipping on pool decks and during water aerobics. **Rocky shorelines and creeks.** Thicker soles with toe protection and good grip. Lace-ups work well here. See the [best women's water shoes for rocky beaches](/clothing-footwear/best-women-s-water-shoes-for-rocky-beaches).

**Kayaking, paddleboarding and boating.** Shoes that stay on, drain and give grip on a wet deck. Neoprene booties add warmth in cool water.

**River hiking and canyoneering.** A rugged shoe with a sticky sole, secure lacing and drain ports. These are closer to hiking shoes than casual water shoes.

**Fishing and wading.** Boots or shoes with felt or rubber soles for slippery rocks; check local rules about felt soles, which are banned in some areas to prevent the spread of invasive species.

## Budget

Water shoes cost less than hiking footwear, and you can find decent pairs at low prices. The [best water shoes under $20](/clothing-footwear/best-water-shoes-under-20) and [under $50](/clothing-footwear/best-water-shoes-under-50) lists show what you get at each level. Budget shoes usually have thin soles and less durable uppers, and are fine for beach days and casual use. Mid-priced shoes add a stronger sole, better drainage and more comfortable footbeds. For heavy use, consider spending more, since a shoe that falls apart on a river trip is a hazard.

## Water shoes versus other footwear

Water shoes are not a replacement for hiking footwear. They have little arch support and thin soles, which makes them tiring on long walks. For long trails with stream crossings, trail runners that drain and dry quickly may be better; see [hiking boots vs hiking shoes vs trail runners](/clothing-footwear/hiking-boots-vs-hiking-shoes-vs-trail-runners) and [waterproof vs non-waterproof hiking footwear](/clothing-footwear/waterproof-vs-non-waterproof-hiking-shoes) for the comparison. For short crossings or a day on the water, water shoes win on drying and comfort. Many campers pack a pair of water shoes as camp shoes and for shower houses, which keeps feet protected and lets hiking footwear dry.

## Safety on wet surfaces

Wet rock, algae and mud are among the slipperiest surfaces you will meet outdoors, and the right shoe cuts the risk but does not remove it. Sticky rubber outsoles grip wet stone far better than hard plastic, and a lugged pattern improves grip on mud. Even so, step on the rough, textured parts of rock, avoid green or shiny patches and keep your weight centered over your feet. In current, face upstream, move one foot at a time and keep three points of contact if the water is knee deep or more. A pair of water shoes does not make fast water safe, and you should wear a life jacket for boating and swimming in currents.

Think about what is underfoot that you cannot see. Shells, broken glass, fishing hooks, sea urchins and sharp rocks injure bare feet every summer, and a thick sole is the best defense. Hot sand and pavement can burn skin in minutes, since dark surfaces in strong sun can reach well above 120 degrees Fahrenheit, which is hot enough to blister. In cold water, feet lose sensation and you may not notice a cut, so check your feet afterward. Keep a small first aid kit in your bag and clean any wound promptly, because water carries bacteria.

## Water shoes for kids and toddlers

Children spend more time in the water and put more wear on their shoes. For kids, choose a secure closure that they can operate, such as a hook-and-loop strap or a drawcord with a toggle, and a toe cap to protect against stubs. The sole should be flexible and grippy, and the fit snug enough that the shoe stays on in the waves. Check the toe room every few weeks, since feet grow fast, and avoid hand-me-downs that are stretched out.

Toddlers need especially soft, flexible shoes with room to wiggle toes. Bright colors help you spot a shoe that comes off in the water. Look at the best water shoes for toddlers and the best water shoes for kids lists for models designed for small feet. Make it a habit to rinse and dry the shoes after use, which keeps them from smelling and makes them last through a summer of play.

## Wide feet, high arches and special fit

Water shoes use stretchy uppers, which accommodate many foot shapes, but wide feet can still feel squeezed. Look for a wide toe box, an adjustable closure and a model sold in wide sizes, such as those in the best water shoes for men wide feet list. People with high arches benefit from a molded footbed or a removable insole that supports the arch, and those with flat feet may prefer a firmer midsole. If you use orthotics, check that the shoe has a removable insole and enough volume.

Walking on wet surfaces with poor support can aggravate foot pain, so do not use thin aqua socks for long walks if you have plantar fasciitis or other conditions. Choose a shoe with some structure, and give your feet a break. If you are on your feet for hours, bring a second pair of dry shoes to change into and let the first pair dry.

## A quick buying checklist

Before you buy, run through a short list.

1. **Surface:** sand and pool, rocky shore or river bed?
2. **Sole thickness:** thin for flexibility, thick for protection.
3. **Grip:** sticky rubber and multi-directional lugs.
4. **Drainage:** holes or open mesh.
5. **Closure:** laces, drawcord or strap that holds the heel.
6. **Toe protection:** a rubber toe cap for rocks.
7. **Dry time and odor control:** mesh and antimicrobial treatments.
8. **Fit:** snug, with room for toes and no pressure points.
9. **Budget:** match the price to how often you will use them.
10. **Care:** removable insoles and easy rinsing.

If a shoe checks most of these boxes for your use, it is probably a good match. Try them on, walk around and, if you can, test them in a sink or pool before the trip.

## Three trips, three pairs

**A week at a pool and beach resort.** The main needs are protection from hot decks and sand and a shoe that dries quickly between swims. A pair of lightweight slip-on water shoes with a thin, grippy sole and a mesh upper is perfect. It packs flat, costs little and leaves room in the suitcase. A second pair is unnecessary, and a thin pair works in a swimming pool for aqua aerobics too.

**A weekend of kayaking on a cold lake.** Water is in the 50s and your feet will spend hours wet. A neoprene bootie or a closed water shoe with a thicker sole keeps feet warmer, and a snug fit stops it from slipping off when you climb out. Pack a dry pair of socks and shoes for camp. A mesh shoe would drain well but leave feet cold, so the extra insulation matters more than drying speed.

**A day in a slot canyon with several wading sections.** You need grip on wet rock, protection from sharp stone and a shoe that drains and stays on in current. A lace-up hybrid water shoe with a sticky sole, a toe cap and a secure fit is the right tool. It weighs more and costs more than beach shoes, and it takes the abuse. Bring a spare pair of socks for the walk out, and rinse the shoes afterward to remove sand and grit.

## Packing and travel

Water shoes are a smart travel item. They pack flat, weigh a few ounces and serve as pool shoes, shower shoes and camp slippers. Pack them in a mesh or plastic bag, separate from clean clothes, and let them dry before you pack them. A sprinkle of baking soda inside the shoes helps with odor on long trips. If you will be moving between water and hiking, wear them to the trailhead and carry your hiking footwear, or the reverse, and use the shoes as camp shoes in the evening.

On airplanes, wear the bulkiest pair or pack them in a carry-on so they do not get lost. In campgrounds with public showers, water shoes protect against fungus and slippery floors, which makes them handy for RV and tent campers alike. Keep a pair by the door of the camper or tent, where they are easy to grab for a quick walk to the water or the bathhouse. They are one of the cheapest and most versatile pieces of gear you can own.

## When to replace water shoes

Water shoes wear out faster than regular footwear because they are in contact with abrasive surfaces, salt, chlorine and strong sun. Replace them when the sole is worn smooth and loses grip, when the upper is torn, when the midsole has compressed so that the foot feels flat on the ground or when odor that you cannot wash out persists. Cracking rubber, peeling soles and loose seams are signs that the glue has failed. A broken shoe is a safety issue on a wet rock, so retire it before it fails at a bad moment.

Rotating two pairs extends life, since each pair gets time to dry completely between uses. Keep a worn pair for dirty jobs such as rinsing fish or walking in mud, and a better pair for travel. With basic care, a good pair of water shoes lasts several seasons.

## Care

Rinse water shoes in fresh water after use in salt, chlorine or muddy water. Dry them in the shade, since strong sun can degrade rubber and glue. Wash them occasionally with mild soap and a soft brush to reduce odor, and let them dry completely. Remove insoles to dry separately. Avoid hot dryers. Store them in a ventilated place, and replace them when the sole is worn smooth or the upper is torn.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Wearing cotton sneakers in water and carrying them wet.
- Buying shoes too loose, which slip in current.
- Choosing thin soles for sharp rocks.
- Leaving wet shoes in a bag and getting odor.
- Using a water sandal for a long hike.
- Ignoring local rules on felt soles.
- Forgetting to rinse salt and chlorine.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between water shoes and sandals?
Water shoes cover more of the foot and protect the toes, and sandals have open straps and dry faster. For rocky ground, a closed shoe is safer, and for hot weather and rafting, a sandal is cooler. Choose by the surface.

### Can I hike in water shoes?
For short, easy walks, yes. For long hikes, they lack support and cushioning. A trail runner that drains well is a better choice, or a hybrid water-hiking shoe built for trail use.

### How tight should water shoes be?
Snug, with the heel held securely and a little toe room. Your foot slides in water, and a loose shoe can slip off. Use a drawcord or strap to adjust, and try them wet if possible.

### How do I keep water shoes from smelling?
Rinse them after use, dry them completely in the shade and wash them occasionally with mild soap. Remove insoles to dry. Avoid sealing them in a bag while wet.

### Are neoprene water shoes warmer?
Yes, they hold a thin layer of water that warms from your body heat and insulate against cold water. They dry slowly and can feel hot in summer. Choose neoprene for cold water and mesh for warm.

### Do water shoes work for kids?
Yes. Kids need protection from rocks and hot sand, and a secure fit that does not slip. Choose adjustable closures and a little room to grow. Check fit often because kids grow quickly.
