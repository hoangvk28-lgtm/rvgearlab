# How to Store Drinking Water at a Campsite

Store drinking water in clean, food-grade containers with tight lids, keep them cool and out of direct sun, label them with the fill date, and plan on at least 1 gallon per person per day for drinking and basic cooking (2 gallons if you also wash dishes and brush teeth from the same supply). Treated tap water in a sealed food-grade container stays safe for about six months if it is kept cool and dark, but at a campsite you should aim to use it within a few days and refill from a known safe source. Collapsible jugs of 2.5 to 7 gallons are the practical choice for car camping.

Water is the heaviest and most important thing you carry, at 8.3 pounds per gallon, and the easiest to mishandle. A jug left in the sun grows algae and warms into something no one wants to drink, and a container that once held something else can taint it for good. Here is how to estimate how much you need, choose containers, keep water clean and safe, treat it if you must, and handle the cold and the heat.

## How much water do you need?

Plan by activity, not by habit.

| Use | Approx. per person per day |
|---|---|
| Drinking, moderate weather | 0.5 to 1 gallon (2 to 4 liters) |
| Drinking, hot weather or hiking | 1 to 1.5 gallons (4 to 6 liters) |
| Cooking and coffee | 0.25 to 0.5 gallon |
| Dishes and cleanup (minimal) | 0.25 to 0.5 gallon |
| Brushing teeth and hand washing | 0.25 gallon |
| Washing with a camp shower | 1 to 2 gallons per shower |

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention advises storing at least 1 gallon of water per person per day for emergencies, enough for three days, and more if you can manage it. For camping, a good starting figure is 1 to 2 gallons per person per day, depending on how much you cook, wash and sweat. A family of four on a three-night trip with a spigot nearby might carry 10 gallons for drinking and cooking and refill as needed. Without a spigot, plan on 2 gallons per person per day, or 24 gallons for the same family over three days, which is 200 pounds of water.

At a campground with potable water, you can carry less and refill, but have enough capacity for a day's use in case the spigot is far, frozen or turned off. At a dispersed site, bring everything you need, plus a reserve.

## Choosing containers

| Container type | Typical size | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rigid food-grade jug with spigot | 3 to 7 gallons | Sturdy, easy to pour, stackable | Bulky when empty |
| Collapsible water container | 2.5 to 10 gallons | Folds flat, light | Can puncture, harder to clean, may sag |
| Water bladder or reservoir | 1 to 3 liters | Hands-free drinking | Needs frequent cleaning |
| Single-use plastic jugs | 1 gallon | Cheap, convenient | Thin, not for long reuse |
| Stainless steel bottles | 0.5 to 1.5 liters | Durable, no taste | Heavy, expensive |
| Bulk water cubes | 5 to 7 gallons | Compact, stackable | Bulky when empty |

The CDC recommends food-grade containers for storing drinking water, which you can find at camping and surplus stores. It cautions against containers that have held toxic chemicals, including old bleach bottles, and against milk and juice jugs, which are hard to clean fully and break down. Look for labels such as food-grade, BPA-free and NSF or FDA compliant, and check the cap, which should seal firmly. A spigot or spout makes pouring easier and keeps hands out of the water.

For car camping, a mix often works best: one or two large rigid or semi-rigid jugs for the main supply, a collapsible container for refilling, and personal bottles for the day. ## Keeping water clean in storage

Water stays safe when you keep contamination out and slow bacterial growth.

1. **Start with a clean container.** Wash it with hot water and dish soap, rinse thoroughly and, if it will hold drinking water for days, sanitize it according to the container maker or health authority guidance. Let it air dry.
2. **Fill from a safe source.** Use municipal tap water or another potable supply. If the source is uncertain, treat the water before storing it.
3. **Cap it tightly** and avoid touching the inside of the lid or spout.
4. **Label it with the date.** The CDC suggests marking containers as drinking water and noting the storage date, and replacing stored water every six months for long-term supplies.
5. **Store it cool and dark.** Heat and light promote bacterial growth and algae, and heat can also cause plastic to leach taste. A shaded spot, a cooler or a vehicle's shaded footwell is better than a sunny picnic table.
6. **Keep it off the ground and away from chemicals.** Do not store water near fuel, pesticides or cleaning products, which can permeate some plastics.
7. **Use clean dispensing habits.** Pour, do not dip cups into the container, and wash hands before handling.
8. **Rinse and dry between trips.** A damp, capped container grows mold and a musty smell.

## Treating water when you are unsure

If the source is not known to be safe, or if you collect water from a stream or lake, treat it. Several methods exist, and they differ in what they remove.

| Method | What it handles | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Boiling | Bacteria, viruses, protozoa | A rolling boil for at least one minute, longer at altitude |
| Filters (hollow fiber, pump, squeeze) | Bacteria, protozoa, sediment | Check the pore size and the manufacturer's claims, since most filters do not remove viruses |
| Purifiers (filter plus chemical or UV) | Bacteria, protozoa, viruses | Follow the instructions and check the certification |
| Chemical tablets | Bacteria, viruses, many protozoa | Contact time varies from 30 minutes to hours |
| Household bleach | Many pathogens | Follow label directions, and let the water stand at least 30 minutes |

For emergencies or when other options are unavailable, the CDC says to follow the instructions on the label of unscented household bleach, and to let the treated water stand for at least 30 minutes before drinking. As a general rule from university extension guidance, about 8 drops of regular unscented bleach per gallon of clear water, or 16 drops for cloudy water, is commonly cited, but bleach strengths vary, so the label is the authority. Filter cloudy water through a clean cloth first. Do not use scented or color-safe bleach.

Treating water is more about the source than the container. A treated and stored supply is only as safe as the container it is in.

## Hot weather, cold weather and altitude

**Heat.** Warm water tastes poor and breeds bacteria faster. Keep containers in the shade, wrap them in a damp cloth or an insulated sleeve and consider freezing a jug at home to act as an ice block in the cooler, which melts into cold drinking water. Do not leave jugs in a hot car.

**Cold.** Water expands as it freezes and can split rigid containers and burst spigots. In freezing weather, keep water in the tent or vehicle cabin, store containers upside down so ice forms at the bottom first, insulate them or fill them partly. A frozen jug can also be thawed slowly inside a warm space, never near a flame.

**Altitude.** Water behaves the same way, but you drink more at altitude, so plan for 25 to 50 percent more, and boiling requires a longer time to be effective, about three minutes above 6,500 feet according to common public health guidance.

## Dispensing and campsite habits

Setting up a water station keeps the camp tidy and the supply clean. Place a large jug with a spigot on a table or a stable surface, with a drip tray or a rock bed beneath it to prevent mud. Hang a bar of soap or a pump bottle by the spigot and a wash basin below it for hand washing, which separates washing water from drinking water. Use separate containers for drinking water and for dishwater. Mark them clearly so that a child does not drink from the wrong one. Do not pour used water on the ground within 200 feet of streams and lakes where rules require it, and strain food scraps out of dishwater before dispersing it.

## A worked water budget for a four-person weekend

Suppose a family of four camps two nights at a campground with a single standpipe 150 feet from the site. They arrive Friday evening and leave Sunday afternoon, and the weather is warm.

**Drinking.** Four people at about 0.75 gallon each over roughly 2.5 days is 7.5 gallons.

**Cooking and coffee.** Two dinners, two breakfasts and a few pots of coffee use about 0.4 gallon per person per day, or about 4 gallons total.

**Dishes.** One basin of wash water and one of rinse water per meal for five meals comes to about 5 gallons, though much of that can come from the same fill, so call it 3 gallons.

**Hands and teeth.** About 0.25 gallon per person per day, or 2.5 gallons.

**Reserve.** One extra 2 gallons for emergencies and the trip home.

The total is about 19 gallons, or roughly 160 pounds. They do not need to carry that all at once. They bring two 5 gallon containers with spigots, one 2.5 gallon collapsible jug and four personal bottles, for 14.5 gallons of capacity. They fill everything at home or at the standpipe on arrival, and refill the collapsible jug once a day. The family keeps one 5 gallon container for drinking and cooking only, labeled with a marker, and the other for washing. Because the standpipe is a walk away, they leave the jugs partly full and carry the collapsible one to refill. This arrangement keeps drinking water separate from wash water, keeps the weight manageable and gives a reserve if the standpipe is shut off for maintenance.

## Cleaning and sanitizing your containers

A container that looks clean can still carry biofilm, a slimy layer of bacteria that clings to the walls, especially in collapsible jugs and bladders with folds. Establish a cleaning routine:

1. **After each trip, empty the container** and rinse it with warm water.
2. **Wash with mild dish soap** and a bottle brush where the opening allows. For collapsible containers, fill with soapy water, shake, and drain through the spout.
3. **Rinse thoroughly** until no soap remains.
4. **Sanitize periodically,** using a solution recommended by the manufacturer or by public health guidance, then rinse again. Do not mix cleaning products, and never combine bleach with other chemicals.
5. **Dry completely.** Prop the container open, upside down on a rack, or hang it by its handle. A fully dry container stores without a musty smell. Many collapsible containers can be dried by blowing air through or standing them open in the sun for a short time, but long sun exposure can degrade plastic.
6. **Store with the cap off or loose** so that air circulates, and keep it in a clean, dry place.

If a container develops a persistent smell, cloudiness, slime or a taste, retire it. Replacing a $15 jug is cheaper than a stomach illness on a camping trip.

## Water for pets

Dogs need about an ounce of water per pound of body weight per day, more in the heat or when active. Bring a separate bowl, fill it from the same safe supply and do not let pets drink from still pools, where algae and parasites can be a problem. Keep the pet's bowl clean, and wash it daily.

## RV and van water storage

If you travel in an RV or van, the onboard tank is your main storage. Sanitize it at the start of the season by following the manufacturer's steps, and use a food-grade drinking water hose and a filter at the intake. Do not leave water in the tank for weeks in warm weather without using it. Keep a portable container as a backup in case the pump fails or the tank is contaminated. In freezing conditions, drain and winterize or heat the tank and lines. Many RVers use the tank for washing and a separate jug for drinking and cooking, which gives a safe reserve and a clearer taste.

## Quick checklist before you leave home

Wash and dry every container, fill the main jug from your own tap if the campground supply is uncertain, label the date, pack a funnel or a spout adapter for refilling collapsible jugs, bring a spare cap, a small bottle of unscented bleach or a set of purification tablets as a backup and a filter for stream water. Freeze one jug to double as ice. Check that your spigots do not drip, and carry a roll of tape or a spare gasket for the leaks that appear on bumpy roads.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Using containers that held chemicals.** Taste and toxins can remain.
- **Leaving jugs in the sun.** Heat promotes growth and degrades plastic.
- **Dipping cups into the container.** It introduces bacteria.
- **Skipping the date label.** You will not remember how old the water is.
- **Underestimating how much you need.** Plan for drinking, cooking and washing.
- **Ignoring freezing.** Rigid jugs can split.
- **Treating water but not cleaning the container.** Recontamination is common.
- **Using scented bleach or the wrong dose.** Follow the label.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long can I store drinking water in a jug at a campsite?
Treated, potable water in a clean, sealed, food-grade container kept cool and dark can last months, and public health guidance suggests replacing long-term stored water every six months. At a campsite, plan to use it within a few days, since heat and handling shorten safe storage.

### Can I reuse a milk jug or a juice bottle for water?
Health authorities advise against milk and juice jugs because they are difficult to clean fully and the plastic degrades. Use food-grade containers made for water storage. Single-use water bottles can be reused briefly but are not a long-term solution.

### How do I keep water cold in hot weather?
Keep the jug in the shade, wrap it in a damp towel or insulated cover and place a frozen jug in your cooler to act as ice. You can also store the container in the vehicle footwell, away from the sun, or in a cooler with ice.

### How much water should I bring for a weekend trip?
Plan 1 to 2 gallons per person per day depending on whether there is a water source nearby. A family of four for a two-night trip might bring 10 to 16 gallons without a spigot, plus extra for the drive and emergencies.

### Can I use bleach to purify water at the campsite?
In an emergency, you can use unscented household bleach following the label's instructions, and let the water stand at least 30 minutes. Extension guidance commonly cites about 8 drops per gallon for clear water, but strengths vary, so follow the label.

### What is the best container for car camping?
A 5 to 7 gallon rigid or semi-rigid container with a spigot is a practical main supply, with a collapsible jug for refills and personal bottles for the day. For backpacking, choose lightweight collapsible containers.
