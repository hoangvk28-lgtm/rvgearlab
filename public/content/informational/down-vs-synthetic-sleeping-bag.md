# Down vs Synthetic Sleeping Bags

Down is the warmest insulation for its weight and packs smallest, and it lasts for decades with care, but it loses most of its insulating power when it gets wet and costs more. Synthetic insulation is cheaper, keeps much of its warmth when damp and is easier to wash and dry, but it is heavier, bulkier and wears out faster. Choose down for backpacking and dry cold, and synthetic for car camping, wet climates, kids and budgets.

The decision is rarely about a single winner. It depends on where you camp, how you carry the bag, how you care for gear and how much you are willing to spend. This guide explains how each material works, compares them on warmth, weight, packed size, wet performance, durability, cost and ethics, and ends with a short list of questions that settle most cases.

## How each insulation works

Both insulations work the same way: they trap a layer of still air close to your body. Warmth comes from the loft, the thickness of the trapped air layer, not from the material itself.

**Down** is the soft, three-dimensional cluster found under the outer feathers of ducks and geese. Each cluster has thousands of fine filaments radiating from a center, which interlock to trap air very efficiently. Down quality is measured as fill power, the volume in cubic inches that one ounce of down occupies. Higher fill power means larger clusters and more warmth per ounce, as described in our guide to [what down fill power is](/sleep-gear/what-is-down-fill-power).

**Synthetic insulation** uses polyester fibers, spun finer than a hair, in either short staple batts that mimic down's structure or long continuous filaments that are stitched or laminated in layers. The fibers hold air in the gaps between them. Premium synthetics use very fine fibers and tricks like hollow cores to approach down's efficiency, but none matches it by weight.

## Warmth for weight and packed size

Down wins here by a wide margin. A quality 800 fill power down bag rated to 20 degrees Fahrenheit may weigh about 2 pounds and pack to the size of a loaf of bread. A synthetic bag at the same rating typically weighs 3 to 3.5 pounds and packs into a space about 40 to 60 percent larger. For a backpacker counting ounces, that difference is large. For a car camper, who has plenty of trunk space, it barely matters.

| Feature | Down | Synthetic |
|---|---|---|
| Warmth per ounce | Excellent | Good |
| Typical weight at 20F | 1.8 to 2.5 lb | 3 to 4 lb |
| Packed size | Small | 40 to 60 percent larger |
| Wet performance | Poor unless treated | Good, keeps much of its warmth |
| Dry time | Slow | Fast |
| Durability over time | Very long if cared for | Shorter, loses loft sooner |
| Price | Higher | Lower |
| Care | Specialized wash | Easy |
| Allergy risk | Some people react | Hypoallergenic |

Quick note on fill power: a 650 fill bag needs more down by weight to reach the same warmth as an 850 fill bag. The comparison above assumes high-quality down. Lower-quality down closes the weight gap.

## Wet weather performance

This is the main reason people pick synthetic. When down gets wet, the clusters collapse and clump, and the bag loses most of its loft and warmth until it dries, which can take hours or days. Synthetic fibers do not absorb much water and keep their shape, so a wet synthetic bag still insulates, though not as well as a dry one.

Treated down, often sold as hydrophobic or water-resistant down, has a durable water-repellent finish applied to the clusters. It slows water absorption and dries faster, which helps in light moisture and condensation, but it is not waterproof, and it does not make down behave like synthetic in a soaked bag. In practice, wet conditions threaten down mostly through condensation, damp tents and sweat over several nights, rather than a single dunk. A waterproof stuff sack, a good tent and a bag shell with a water-resistant treatment reduce the risk. ## Durability and lifespan

Down is remarkably durable. A well-cared-for down bag can keep its loft for 15 to 20 years or more, because the clusters spring back after compression. Synthetic insulation gradually loses loft as the fibers break down with repeated compression and washing, so a synthetic bag may feel noticeably cooler after five to ten years of regular use. Because down lasts longer, its higher price per bag is partly offset over the life of the product.

Both materials suffer from long-term compression in a stuff sack. Store either type loose in a large breathable bag, as described in how to store a sleeping bag. Washing helps both, and the right method matters; see how to wash a sleeping bag.

## Cost

Down bags cost more upfront. A good-quality 20 degree down bag can cost two to four times as much as a comparable synthetic bag, and premium 850 fill goose down models reach well into the high hundreds of dollars. Synthetic bags in the same temperature range span the budget end to the middle. Over time, down's lifespan can reduce the cost per night, but only for those who use the bag often.

For budget shoppers, the best affordable backpacking sleeping bags lean synthetic, and cheap down bags often use lower fill power and heavier shells. For the premium end, see the [best packable down sleeping bags](/sleep-gear/best-packable-down-sleeping-bags) lists.

## Allergies, ethics and sourcing

Some people react to down because of dust or residual proteins, though modern washing reduces this, and a quality bag from a reputable maker is usually well cleaned. Synthetics are free of this issue. On ethics, many brands now certify their down under standards such as the Responsible Down Standard, which prohibits live plucking and force-feeding, and track the supply chain. Look for such certification if it matters to you. Synthetics are petroleum-based, and a growing number of brands use recycled polyester, which reduces the footprint but does not remove it.

## Which bag fits which trip

**Backpacking and bikepacking.** Down is the usual choice because of its weight and packed size, with synthetic favored for wet regions like the Pacific Northwest and on canoe trips where drowning a bag is possible.

**Car camping.** Synthetic wins on cost and convenience. A big rectangular synthetic bag is simple to wash and forgiving, and weight does not matter. ## Hybrid designs

Some bags combine the two. A down bag may use synthetic insulation in the footbox and the lower half, where moisture and compression are greatest, and down on top. Others use treated down throughout. A few use a synthetic liner against the skin. Hybrids give a compromise of weight and wet performance at an intermediate price. They are worth considering if you camp in mixed conditions and want some insurance.

## Care differences that affect your choice

Down needs gentle handling: wash it with a down-specific cleaner in a front-loading machine, dry it on low heat with clean tennis balls or dryer balls to break up clumps, and make sure it is completely dry before storing. A bag that is even slightly damp can mildew. Synthetic can be washed in a regular machine with mild detergent and dried on low. If you are not willing to do the careful drying, choose synthetic.

Think also about daily handling: a down bag is more easily damaged by sharp zippers and rough stuffing, though modern shells are strong. Keep either type clean with a liner to reduce washing frequency; see do sleeping bag liners actually add warmth.

## Shell fabrics and linings matter more than they look

The insulation sits inside a shell, and the shell controls how well the bag handles moisture, abrasion and drafts. Lightweight backpacking bags use 10 to 20 denier nylon or polyester with a durable water-repellent finish. It is thin and light and wears faster, so keep it away from rough ground and sharp zippers. Car-camping bags use heavier 40 to 75 denier fabrics, and some use a flannel or cotton lining that feels cozy on the skin but absorbs moisture and dries slowly. Synthetic linings such as polyester taffeta dry quickly and are the better choice for backpacking.

A water-resistant shell is a good partner for down, because it slows the entry of condensation and splashes. Some premium bags use membrane laminates in the shell that are close to waterproof, which gives down a real advantage in damp tents at the cost of breathability. If you camp where frost builds inside the tent walls, a shell with a good finish makes a difference in how much moisture the insulation takes up over several nights.

Look at the baffle design as well. Box baffles hold down in individual chambers so it cannot shift, and sewn-through seams create cold spots where the shell is stitched directly to the lining. Synthetic bags often use offset or shingled layers to cover seams. For a three-season bag, sewn-through is fine, and for deep cold a box baffle or offset layering matters. These details explain why two bags with the same stated rating can feel different on a cold night.

## Real-world scenarios

**Scenario one: a first-time backpacker with a limited budget.** Two or three trips a year, summer and early fall, mostly dry weather, a pack that already feels heavy. A synthetic 30 degree bag at a modest price gives reliable warmth, handles a surprise shower and survives a washing machine. Spending a few hundred dollars more on down would save about a pound, and the beginner would likely prefer to upgrade the pad and the pack first.

**Scenario two: a thru-hiker covering hundreds of miles.** Every ounce is felt hundreds of thousands of times. A 20 degree down bag with 800 fill or higher, a water-resistant shell and a waterproof liner for the stuff sack is the standard. The thru-hiker accepts the cost and the careful drying routine in return for a lighter pack and a bag that lasts several years of daily use.

**Scenario three: a family that car camps in the Southeast.** Humid nights, occasional storms, kids who treat the bag like a toy. Synthetic bags in a rectangular shape make sense. They can go in the washing machine, dry in a standard dryer and take occasional abuse. Weight and packed size do not matter because the trunk is big enough.

**Scenario four: a winter camper in the Rockies.** Dry cold, long nights, a bag that may spend several nights in a row in frosty conditions. A high fill power down bag rated to 0 degrees with a vapor-friendly shell is the lightest option, but the camper must manage frost by airing the bag each day and using a vapor barrier liner if needed. Someone who cannot guarantee daily drying time might choose synthetic.

## A short decision checklist

Answer these in order, and stop when the answer is clear.

1. Will you carry the bag more than a short walk? If yes, down becomes attractive.
2. Do you camp mostly in wet, humid or marine climates, or on the water? If yes, lean synthetic or hybrid.
3. Is your budget below a few hundred dollars? Choose synthetic.
4. Will you wash and dry the bag carefully, and store it loose? If not, choose synthetic.
5. Does anyone using the bag have a down allergy? Choose synthetic.
6. Do you want one bag for ten years or more? Down offers better lifespan.

If your answers split, a hybrid bag or two bags, one of each, may be the sensible solution. Many campers own a synthetic bag for car camping and wet trips and a down bag for backpacking.

## Using the bag well, whichever you choose

Habits matter as much as materials. Shake out the bag when you set up camp and let it loft for 20 to 30 minutes before use, because a freshly unpacked bag needs time to expand. Sleep with a clean, dry base layer to reduce body oils in the insulation. Open the bag and air it each morning, and hang it in the sun at lunch if the day allows. Keep it off the ground and out of wet tent corners. A simple stuff sack with a compression strap is fine for the trail, but unpack the bag as soon as you get home.

## Temperature ratings are the same either way

A 20 degree down bag and a 20 degree synthetic bag are supposed to perform the same in the standard test. The difference lies in weight, volume and how they behave when wet or old. For how ratings work, see [sleeping bag temperature ratings explained](/sleep-gear/sleeping-bag-temperature-ratings-explained).

## What happens to each type on a long trip

Over several nights, moisture accumulates in any bag. You exhale, perspire and bring humidity from the tent into the insulation, and a few nights of this is manageable for a synthetic bag and a challenge for a down bag. After four or five nights of damp, cold weather, a down bag often feels thinner in the morning because the clusters have taken on water and clumped. The fix is routine: turn the bag inside out and air it in sun or wind when possible, shake it, and avoid cinching the hood over your face. A synthetic bag shrugs off the same conditions and may feel only slightly heavier.

This is also where the shell and liner pay off. A liner adds warmth and keeps the bag cleaner, so you wash it less often. A vapor barrier liner is a specialized tool for deep cold and expeditions, and it holds moisture next to your body instead of letting it enter the insulation. For most campers, simple habits and a good tent matter more.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Buying a down bag for a wet kayaking or canoe trip without waterproof storage.
- Choosing cheap low-fill-power down and expecting premium weight and warmth.
- Compressing a bag in its stuff sack for months.
- Machine drying a down bag on high heat.
- Washing a down bag with regular detergent.
- Believing that "water-resistant down" is waterproof.
- Overspending on ultralight down for a car-camping trip.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is down always warmer than synthetic?
Not at the same weight and rating. At a given temperature rating, both should keep you equally warm, but the down bag will be lighter and smaller. A bag's warmth depends on the amount and quality of insulation, not just the material.

### Does hydrophobic down solve the wet problem?
It helps. Treated down absorbs water more slowly and dries faster, which is useful against damp air and light moisture. It is not waterproof, and a soaked bag will still lose much of its loft. Protect it with a good tent and a waterproof stuff sack.

### How long do down and synthetic bags last?
A well-cared-for down bag can last 15 to 20 years or more. Synthetic bags typically lose noticeable loft after 5 to 10 years of regular use. Storage and washing practices affect both.

### Can I wash a down bag at home?
Yes, in a front-loading washer with a down-specific cleaner, rinse twice and dry on low heat with dryer balls until completely dry. A top-loader with a center agitator can tear baffles. See our guide on washing sleeping bags for the steps.

### Which is better for kids?
Synthetic, in most cases. It is easy to wash, tolerates spills and costs less for a bag that will be outgrown. Down makes sense only for older children on frequent backpacking trips.

### Are synthetic bags worth it for backpacking?
Yes, in wet climates, on budgets or for those who want a worry-free bag. The weight and bulk are the cost, which many beginners accept to save money. As trips get longer, down's advantages grow.
