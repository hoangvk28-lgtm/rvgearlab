# Camping Cot Sizes and Weight Capacity Explained

A camping cot's weight capacity is the maximum static load it is rated to hold, usually 250 to 600 pounds, and its size is the length and width of the sleeping surface, typically 74 to 84 inches long and 25 to 35 inches wide for a single. Choose a cot whose rated capacity is at least 50 to 100 pounds above your weight plus gear, whose length is at least 6 inches longer than you and whose width suits how you sleep. Wider and heavier cots are more comfortable and sturdier, and also bulkier and costlier.

Cot numbers come from manufacturers, not from an independent standard, so a "300 pound" cot from one maker may not be equivalent to a "300 pound" cot from another. Understanding how capacity is stated, what frame, fabric and leg design carry the load and how size affects comfort and fit in a tent helps you choose a cot that lasts and sleeps well. This guide covers capacity ratings, size categories, frame and fabric, height, weight and packed size, tent fit and safe use.

## What weight capacity really means

Weight capacity is the maximum load the maker says the cot can carry. It is generally a **static** rating: the weight of a person lying still. Moving, sitting hard on one end, climbing in and out or kids jumping puts dynamic loads that can be two to three times the static weight for a moment. A cot rated at 300 pounds is designed for a person under 300 pounds lying still, not for a 280 pound person who sits down hard on the edge.

Because makers set their own test methods, some include a safety margin and some do not. A reasonable approach is to choose a cot with a capacity at least 50 to 100 pounds above your total weight, including a pad and bedding, and more if you are tall or shift around. Heavier users and couples should look at higher-capacity models. A cot that is "rated for 450 pounds" is not necessarily better than one rated for 300, but the higher rating generally comes with a stronger frame and thicker fabric.

## Size: length, width and height

**Length.** Choose a cot at least 6 inches longer than your height so your head and feet do not hang over. Most single cots are 74 to 84 inches (6.2 to 7 feet) long. Sleepers above 6 feet should pick an XL cot of about 80 to 84 inches.

**Width.** Single cots run 25 to 35 inches wide. A narrow cot of 25 inches leaves little room to turn, while a 30 inch cot gives more freedom, and 35 inches or more is roomy. Wider cots are heavier and larger when packed. Side sleepers and restless sleepers often prefer the extra width, and options in the [best camping cots for side sleepers](/sleep-gear/best-camping-cots-for-side-sleepers) list tend to be wider and use thicker pads.

**Couples** can use a double cot, which is 45 to 55 inches wide, or two single cots side by side. Double cots are heavy and large, and their capacity rating must cover two people. The [best camping cots for couples](/sleep-gear/best-camping-cots-for-couples) list shows how these designs differ.

**Height** from the ground is typically 7 to 20 inches. A low cot sits closer to the ground, is more stable and fits into lower tents. A higher cot makes it easier to get in and out and creates storage space beneath, but takes more headroom in a tent and may feel less stable. Elevated cots keep you off cold ground, which helps with insulation, though air circulating below can chill you in cold weather.

## Frame materials and design

**Aluminum frames** are light and rustproof, good for backpacking cots and lighter users. They are less stiff than steel at the same weight, so capacity tends to be lower.

**Steel frames** are strong and heavy, the usual choice for high-capacity car-camping cots. Look for powder coating to resist rust. Steel tubing wall thickness and diameter matter.

**Leg design** affects stability. Cots with a cross-brace or X-frame fold easily but can wobble. Cots with straight legs and a rigid frame are more stable. Leg end caps protect tents and prevent sinking into soft ground; wider feet spread the load. For tents with floors, check that leg tips do not poke holes.

**Joints and hinges** are the weak points. Look for riveted or welded joints and thick hinges. A cot that creaks and flexes in the store will do so in the field.

## Fabric and sleeping surface

The sleeping surface is usually polyester or nylon fabric with a denier of 600D or higher for sturdy cots. The fabric is stretched across the frame, and tension affects comfort. A taut surface is firm, while a looser surface sags and feels like a hammock. Look for a double-layer fabric or reinforcement at stress points. Fabric that is too thin tears at the seams, and cheap stitching fails first.

Padding varies. Some cots have a built-in foam pad, but most are bare and rely on a separate pad or mattress. A pad on a cot also provides insulation from cold air below, and a pad with a decent R-value is needed in cool weather. See [camping cot vs sleeping pad](/sleep-gear/camping-cot-vs-sleeping-pad) and [camping cot vs air mattress](/sleep-gear/camping-cot-vs-air-mattress) for the comparison, and what is R-value in a sleeping pad for insulation values.

## Weight and packed size

A cot is the heaviest part of a sleep system, so weight matters if you carry it. Backpacking cots weigh 3 to 6 pounds and pack to the size of a large tent pole bag. Car-camping cots weigh 15 to 40 pounds and fold to about 3 to 4 feet by 6 to 8 inches. Some fold flat like a table, others roll or collapse into a carrying bag.

Packed size matters for car and tent storage. A cot that folds to 40 inches long can lie across a back seat or in a trunk, while a cot that stays 80 inches long needs a roof box or truck bed. Check the packed dimensions against your vehicle and storage space.

## Tent fit and setup

Cots take floor space, and a cot in a small tent leaves almost no room. Count the cot's length and width plus clearance on each side. Our guide on what tent person capacity really means shows how to do the arithmetic. A 4-person tent might fit only two cots with narrow aisles. Measure the tent floor and the cots, and leave at least 12 to 18 inches between cots or between a cot and the wall.

![Hong Kong's style camp bed in villages of Lantau Island, Hong Kong](/images/info/camping-cot-weight-capacity-and-size-1.webp)

Setup can be tricky. Many cots need you to push the legs apart and lock the frame, which takes strength and a little practice. Test the setup at home. A cot that is difficult to set up is likely to be left at home. Some designs use a quick-fold mechanism that can pinch fingers, so keep hands clear.

## Worked examples: matching a cot to a person

**A 6 foot 2 inch, 210 pound camper who sleeps on his back.** Length: at least 6 feet 8 inches, so an 80 inch cot is the starting point and an 84 inch model is better. Capacity: 210 plus pad, bedding and a bag of clothes is about 235 pounds, so a 300 pound cot leaves 65 pounds of margin, a reasonable amount for a back sleeper who does not move much. Width: 30 inches is adequate. A steel-frame standard cot in the 15 to 22 pound range fits the bill.

**A 5 foot 6 inch, 130 pound side sleeper.** Length is not an issue, since a 74 inch cot is sufficient. Width matters more: she needs room for hips and shoulders, so a 30 to 35 inch cot is better than a 25 inch one, plus a pad that cushions the pressure points. Capacity is easy. A lighter cot rated for 250 to 300 pounds works, though the wide models are the main consideration. A 2 inch self-inflating pad on top provides cushion and insulation.

**A couple, 190 and 165 pounds, who want one bed.** The combined weight with bedding is about 380 pounds. A double cot rated for 500 pounds or more is the safe option, and the frame should be steel with wide feet. Check that the tent can take a cot 50 to 55 inches wide and 80 inches long, plus clearance. If space or weight is tight, two single cots rated for 300 pounds each, placed side by side, are cheaper and easier to carry, with a gap in the middle.

**A family with a teen who is 5 foot 10 and 170 pounds.** A standard 300 pound cot at 78 inches is a good fit. Teens are hard on gear: they climb in, flop onto the cot and sit on the edge. Choose one with a strong frame and good joints, and teach them to sit in the center. A cot with a 400 pound rating gives extra margin for the dynamic loads.

## What to check in a store or listing

Not every spec appears on the box, so look for these points.

- **Frame tube diameter and wall thickness**, which indicate stiffness. Thicker is stronger.
- **Fabric denier**, with 600D or higher for sturdy cots, and stitching at the edges.
- **Joint construction**, such as rivets, welds or thick pins and bolts.
- **Leg feet**, wide with rubber caps that will not poke through a floor.
- **Folded dimensions and weight**, to check fit in your vehicle.
- **Warranty and replacement parts**, since a durable cot should be repairable.
- **Setup instructions and photos**, which show whether you can set it up alone.
- **Feedback about stability**, such as wobble, creaking and sag over time.

Sit on the cot in the store if you can, and listen for squeaks. Push on the frame from the side to check for flex. A well-built cot feels solid and stays quiet, and it will be a long-term piece of gear rather than a one-season purchase.

## Cold-weather cot setup

A cot raises you into moving air, so in cold weather the main job is blocking heat loss from below. Lay a closed-cell foam pad or an insulated inflatable pad on the fabric, choosing an R-value that matches the night, and add a wool blanket or an extra pad if it is cold. Hang a blanket or a piece of reflective insulation from the sides of the cot to reduce airflow beneath it, and place a mat or a rug on the floor under the cot. A cot also loses the ground's slight thermal mass, so a sleeping bag with a lower rating may feel colder on a cot than on the ground.

In winter, check that the cot legs do not sink into snow or frozen mud, and place a board or plastic sheet under each leg. Keep metal frames away from skin, as they conduct cold quickly, and avoid touching them with bare hands in freezing temperatures. Use a larger pad width to cover the whole sleeping surface, and make sure the pad does not slip against the fabric, which can be slick.

## When to replace a cot

A cot has a finite life. Replace it when the fabric tears or the stitching at the frame edge starts to unravel, when joints bend or the frame no longer sits level, when rivets loosen or fall out, or when the folding mechanism no longer locks. A bent leg may support you for a night and fail on the next. Do not repair structural failures with tape or glue, since a sudden collapse can injure you. Minor problems such as a loose cap or a squeaky hinge are easy to fix with a replacement part or lubricant.

## Cots in RVs, vans and campsites

Cots are popular with RV and van campers who want extra beds for guests or kids. Measure the floor and the clearance before buying, and check that the folded cot fits a storage bay or a bench. In a van, a low cot slides under a bed platform for storage, and in an RV a folded cot can lean beside a cabinet. At a campsite, put the cot on level ground away from the fire ring and drainage, and use pads under the legs on soft dirt. Wipe the frame after dusty trips, and keep a small bag with a spare cap and a wrench so that a loose leg does not end the night.

## Safety and care

- Place the cot on level, firm ground, and check that the legs are fully extended and locked.
- Do not exceed the weight rating, and avoid standing on the cot.
- Keep children from jumping or climbing on it.
- Inspect the frame, joints and fabric before each trip.
- Dry the cot before storing it, and wipe off dirt and sand.
- Lubricate hinges with a dry lubricant if they stick.
- Store it in a dry place, with the fabric not under tension for long periods.

## Cot versus pad versus air mattress

A cot lifts you off the ground, which keeps you away from damp, bugs and cold, and gives a firm, flat surface. It is heavier and bulkier than a pad. A sleeping pad is light and packs small, but you are on the ground. An air mattress is comfortable and cheap, but cold from below without insulation and may leak. A cot with a pad on top combines the benefits, with a cost in weight.

Choose a cot when you car camp, need to sleep above the ground, have back problems or find getting up from the ground difficult. Choose a pad for backpacking and for minimal weight. For the full comparison, read the related guides above.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Choosing a cot at exactly your weight with no margin.
- Ignoring the width and finding the cot too narrow.
- Forgetting to insulate below the cot in cold weather.
- Buying a cot that does not fit in the tent or vehicle.
- Not testing the setup before the trip.
- Setting the cot on soft ground without wide feet.
- Overlooking the dynamic load when getting in and out.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much weight can a camping cot hold?
Typical ratings run from 225 to 600 pounds, with most adult cots at 300 to 450. The rating is a static load. Choose a cot with at least 50 to 100 pounds of margin above your weight plus gear.

### What size cot do I need?
Pick a length at least 6 inches longer than you are tall, usually 74 to 84 inches, and a width of 25 to 35 inches. Larger sleepers and side sleepers benefit from XL and wide cots.

### Can two people sleep on one cot?
Only on a double cot rated for the combined weight. Single cots are not designed for two. Double cots are heavy and need a large tent, and many couples prefer two single cots.

### Are cots warm enough for cold weather?
A cot lets cold air circulate below you, so you need insulation, such as a pad with a good R-value, a thick blanket or an insulated cover. Without it, a cot can feel colder than the ground.

### How do I keep a cot from sinking into soft ground?
Use cots with wide feet or place a board or a flat plate under each leg. A groundsheet helps in grass, and a sturdy mat spreads the load.

### Will a cot damage my tent floor?
It can if the legs have sharp ends. Choose a cot with rubber caps or wide feet, and place a mat or a piece of cardboard under the legs. Check the tent floor after use.
