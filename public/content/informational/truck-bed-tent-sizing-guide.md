# Truck Bed Tent Sizing Guide by Bed Length

Pick a truck bed tent by measuring your bed, not by guessing from the truck's name. Short beds run about 5.5 feet, standard beds about 6.5 feet, and long beds about 8 feet, and each tent is cut for one of those ranges or for a stated span. A tent that is a foot too long drags on the tailgate side and leaves a gap for rain, while one that is a foot too short will not reach the bed rails.

Because truck tents sit on the bed itself, a bad fit is not a comfort problem, it is a weather and security problem. Most buyers have a fixed bed length and a fixed bed width between the wheel wells, which makes sizing a measuring exercise instead of a judgment call. This guide shows what to measure, how bed lengths vary by truck, how tailgate and bed covers change the numbers, and which spec lines to check on a listing.

## The three bed-length categories

Pickup beds fall into three broad groups, and tent makers design around them. Always confirm against your own truck, because model years and cab configurations shift the numbers by several inches.

| Category | Typical interior bed length | Common examples |
|---|---|---|
| Short bed | 5 ft 2 in to 5 ft 9 in | Crew-cab Tacoma, F-150 5.5 ft, Silverado 5 ft 8 in |
| Standard bed | 6 ft 2 in to 6 ft 9 in | F-150 6.5 ft, Ram 1500 6 ft 4 in, Silverado 6 ft 7 in |
| Long bed | 7 ft 10 in to 8 ft 2 in | F-250 8 ft, Ram 2500 8 ft, Silverado 8 ft |

Mid-size trucks, such as Tacoma, Colorado and Ranger, often have even shorter beds in the 5 to 5.5 foot range. A tent sold for "full-size" beds may not fit these at all.

## What to measure before you shop

Four measurements decide the fit. Take them with a tape measure and write them down.

![Marines loading a folded tent into the bed of a truck](/images/info/truck-bed-tent-sizing-guide-1.webp)

1. **Bed length, floor, with the tailgate closed.** Measure from the inside of the bulkhead (the wall behind the cab) to the inside of the closed tailgate. Tents that use the tailgate up are sized to this number.
2. **Bed length with the tailgate down.** Measure from the bulkhead to the end of the tailgate lying flat. This is the length for tents that extend over the lowered gate.
3. **Width between wheel wells.** This is the narrowest floor width, usually 41 to 51 inches. Air mattresses wider than the gap will ride up over the wells.
4. **Width at the top of the rails.** Many tents wrap over the rail, so this is the width of the fabric footprint.

Keep the numbers in the same units as the listing. If a tent states "fits beds 5.5 to 6.5 feet," that range is meant to cover a short or standard bed with the gate up or down, but verify the claim against the dimension list.

## Tailgate up or down: how it changes the fit

A truck tent either rests with the tailgate closed or encloses the open gate. Tailgate-up designs are simpler and weather-tight. They need a bed length that matches the closed length, and any gap at the back is closed by the fabric wrapping around the gate edge. Tailgate-down designs add roughly 1.5 to 2 feet of length, the depth of the lowered gate, which makes a short bed behave like a standard one and gives two sleepers more legroom.

![Service member working beside a pickup truck bed with a tent loaded](/images/info/truck-bed-tent-sizing-guide-2.webp)

If your truck has a short bed and you want a taller adult to sleep flat, a tailgate-down tent in the range of the [best truck tents for 8 ft beds](/tents-shelter/best-truck-tents-for-8ft-beds) will not fit, but a tent built for standard beds used with the gate down may. Always match the maker's own statement: "Compatible bed length with tailgate down: 6.0 to 6.7 ft" is a testable claim, while "fits most trucks" is not.

## Model-specific fit: F-150, Ram 1500 and others

The most popular full-size trucks offer several bed lengths, so a model name alone cannot confirm fit. For example, an F-150 comes with a 5.5 foot bed, a 6.5 foot bed and an 8 foot bed, depending on cab and year. A Ram 1500 comes with a 5 ft 7 in bed in crew-cab form and a 6 ft 4 in bed in standard form. Tent listings aimed at a specific truck usually target the most common bed, so confirm that your cab and bed combination matches.

Use the model guides as a starting point. The shortlist in the [best truck tents for F150](/tents-shelter/best-truck-tents-for-f150) and the [best truck tents for Ram 1500](/tents-shelter/best-truck-tents-for-ram-1500) highlight listings that name those trucks, but you should still check the bed length against your tape. Newer models also have different tailgates, such as multi-function or split gates, which can interfere with a wrap-around design.

## Bed covers, liners and toolboxes

Accessories change the usable space more than people expect. A tonneau cover must be removed or stowed before the tent goes up, since most tents sit on the bed floor or wrap over the rails. A drop-in bedliner raises the floor slightly and has ridges that print through an air mattress. A spray-in liner is flush and tolerates a foam pad well. Cross-bed toolboxes or bed rails can eat 10 to 20 inches of length.

Check clearance for these items. Tents with fabric that wraps over the rail need free rails, and bed racks or caps interfere with the support structure. If you use a camper shell, a truck tent is the wrong product; look instead at a shell-mounted sleeping platform or an SUV-style tent.

## Floor width, wheel wells and sleeping surface

The wheel wells are the part most buyers forget. The width between them, typically 41 to 51 inches, is the width of a flat sleeping surface. A full-size air mattress at 54 inches will be 3 to 13 inches too wide, and a queen at 60 inches is wider still. Two adults often need to rest one body width partly on the raised wheel-well ledges.

You have a few options. A truck-bed air mattress is shaped with cut-outs for the wells and offers a flat bed with extra width above them. A folding foam pad or a tri-fold mattress can be placed on a board that spans the wells. A cot is another choice, with a height that clears the wells. For two adults, plan on 4 feet of floor width at minimum; with kids or gear, many people extend the sleeping area onto the folded-down gate and sleep across the bed rather than along it.

## Budget, material and weather protection

Truck tents occupy a narrow price band, since the fabric area is modest. The lowest tier uses thin polyester with fiberglass poles, and the main trade-off is durability under wind. A mid-priced model uses a stronger coating and aluminum poles. Prices change often, so compare listings directly. The [best truck tents under $150](/tents-shelter/best-truck-tents-under-150) and [best truck tents under $200](/tents-shelter/best-truck-tents-under-200) lists cover the lower tiers.

Fabric thickness and coating determine weather protection. Look for a fly that reaches the sides of the bed and a floor-less design, since the bed itself is the floor, with an elasticated hem. Check whether the tent has a rainfly or an integrated roof, and how it ties off at the tailgate. An open gap at the gate corners leaks in wind-driven rain, which is why you want a tent with a flap or a cord-lock closure at the back.

## Extras that earn their keep

Extras add real value on a truck tent. An awning or vestibule extends over the tailgate to create a covered entry where you can remove boots or cook out of the rain. Mesh windows with cover flaps provide airflow in summer, and a gear loft stores small items overhead. A zippered tailgate door makes entry simple when the gate is closed.

## Setup and safety on a truck bed

Truck tents introduce risks that ground tents do not. Parking on a slope tilts your sleeping surface, so choose level ground and set the parking brake. Keep sleeping gear away from the exhaust side if you idle the engine, and never run a heater or a vehicle engine with the tent enclosed. Stakes are less necessary, but guylines or ratchet straps keep the tent from flapping in wind.

Pack the tent loosely after a trip, because the fabric spends time in contact with the truck bed, which can be dirty or oily. Wipe the bed before setup and use a ground tarp underneath if there is grit. Cleaning the tent is covered in [how to clean a tent without damaging it](/tents-shelter/how-to-clean-a-tent), and storage in [how to store a tent the right way](/tents-shelter/how-to-store-a-tent). Waterproofing needs are similar to a standard tent; see [how to waterproof a tent](/tents-shelter/how-to-waterproof-a-tent) if the seams begin to leak.

## Sleeping-system math for a truck bed

A truck bed is a small rectangle with two obstructions in it, so it rewards planning. Start with the floor you actually have: a standard bed is about 78 inches long with the gate up, and 41 to 51 inches clear between the wheel wells. Two adults sleeping side by side need at least 48 inches of flat width, which means they straddle the wells unless the mattress is shaped for them. A truck-bed air mattress made for a 6.5 foot bed runs around 66 to 72 inches long and has cut-outs or raised wings that bridge the wells; it is a better match than a standard queen, which is 80 inches long and bunches at the tailgate.

Pad choice is a second decision. Closed-cell foam pads have modest R-values (commonly around 2 for a thin pad), and they cannot leak, so they are the dependable choice for cold nights when a punctured air mattress would be a disaster. Inflatable pads insulate better per ounce, with R-values of 4 or more, but a leak ends the night. Many truck campers carry a foam pad as the base layer and an air mattress on top, which also fills the dips around the wells.

Bedding follows the same logic as any camping trip, but there is a particular cold-air problem under a truck: the steel bed pulls heat out through your pad, and air circulates freely beneath the vehicle. Add a layer of insulation between you and the metal, and use a sleeping bag rated at least 10 degrees below the forecast low. In summer the opposite problem appears: a dark steel bed in the sun radiates heat into the evening, so park in shade and open both end vents.

## Parking, legality and campsite etiquette

A truck tent is a vehicle-based shelter, which changes the rules on where you can use it. Developed campgrounds and national forest dispersed sites generally allow it, since you are camping on the vehicle's parking spot. Rest areas, city streets, big-box store lots and many beaches do not, and municipal ordinances often prohibit sleeping in a vehicle overnight. Check local rules and posted signs before you set up.

Pick a campsite carefully. Look for level ground with a firm base, because a soft shoulder can sink a wheel overnight. Park with the tailgate facing away from the prevailing wind and away from drainage lines. Leave a clear path to the driver's door so that you can leave quickly if a storm approaches, and keep your keys inside the cab where you can reach them from the tent. Avoid parking under dead branches, which fall in wind.

## When a truck tent is the wrong choice

Truck tents suit one to two people who value a quick setup and a dry, level sleeping surface. They are the wrong choice when you need to leave the truck parked at camp while you explore, because the tent ties the vehicle to the campsite and has to be packed away each time you drive. They are also awkward for tall sleepers on short beds, for families with children, and for anyone who wants standing room. In those situations a ground tent, a rooftop tent or an SUV-style tent that attaches to the back of a vehicle offers more flexibility.

Consider your trip style honestly. If you drive to a site, stay two or three nights and cook at the tailgate, a truck tent shines. If you hop between spots daily, the time to unfold and fold a hoop-pole tent on a tall bed becomes a chore. A pop-up design that stays attached is faster but more expensive.

## A quick sizing checklist

- Measure bed length with the tailgate up and down, and bed width at the wheel wells.
- Note your truck's cab, year and bed type; confirm the match to the tent's listed range.
- Decide whether you want to sleep with the gate closed or open.
- Check for toolboxes, liners, bed racks or covers that interfere with the tent.
- Choose an air mattress sized to the wheel-well width, not to the full bed.
- Confirm the tent has a rainfly and a closure at the tailgate.
- Look for a one-person setup description with photos of the assembly sequence.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Buying by truck model name without verifying the bed length.
- Choosing a full-size air mattress that does not fit between the wheel wells.
- Forgetting that a tonneau cover, cap or toolbox blocks a tent that sits on the bed.
- Ignoring the tailgate gap, which is the most common leak point.
- Parking on a slope and sleeping with your feet lower than your head.
- Running the engine or a heater inside an enclosed tent.
- Setting up on a dirty bed without a mat or tarp, which grinds into the fabric.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will a tent for a 6.5 foot bed fit a 5.5 foot bed?
Probably not well. A tent cut for a longer bed overhangs the tailgate end and leaves slack that catches wind. Some tents state a range that spans both lengths, so look for a stated range and confirm with the maker's dimensions. If the range does not include your length, choose a different model.

### Can I use a truck bed tent with a tonneau cover?
You have to remove or fold the cover first, since the tent sits on the bed floor or wraps over the rails. Soft roll-up covers are easier to remove than hard folding ones. If you want to keep the cover on, consider a rooftop tent or a ground tent instead.

### Is a truck tent worth it over a regular tent?
It keeps you off wet, rocky or sloping ground and uses the bed as a flat base, which is a real advantage in muddy or uneven sites. A regular tent is cheaper per square foot and roomier. A truck tent is best for solo travelers and couples who want fast setup and don't need much space.

### How do I stop the tent leaking at the tailgate?
Choose a design with a flap or cord closure at the rear and pull the fabric tight. Check the seams at the gate corners and treat them with seam sealer if needed. Avoid pitching with the open end facing the wind.

### Can two adults sleep in a short-bed truck?
With the tailgate down and a tent that covers it, two average adults can sleep across the bed or lengthwise with their feet over the gate area. Without the gate, a 5.5 foot bed is tight for adults over 5 feet 9 inches. Test the layout at home with a tape measure.

### How do I keep the bed from feeling hard?
A truck-bed air mattress or a thick foam pad evens out ribs and wheel wells. Self-inflating pads work well because they pack small. Add a rug or insulating layer in cold weather, since metal conducts heat away from your body.
