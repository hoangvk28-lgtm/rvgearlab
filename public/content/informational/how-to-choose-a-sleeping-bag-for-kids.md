# How to Choose a Sleeping Bag for Kids

Choose a kids' sleeping bag by temperature rating first, then length, then insulation type and weight. Pick a bag rated 10 degrees Fahrenheit colder than the night's expected low, a length about 6 to 12 inches longer than your child, and synthetic insulation for easy washing. Children lose heat faster than adults, so a pad with enough insulation matters as much as the bag.

Kids' bags have their own quirks: growth, spills, restless sleeping, small bodies that struggle to heat a big bag and manufacturer ratings that are often optimistic. A bag that is too big is cold, one that is too small is tight and a thin pad sends warmth into the ground. This guide covers rating and fit, shape, insulation, features that make a bag work for children, how to extend a bag's life as a child grows and a short comparison of bag types for ages from toddler to teen.

## Why kids need different thinking

Children have a larger surface area compared with their body mass, which means they lose heat more quickly than adults and take longer to warm up. They also move more, which can untuck covers, and they may not tell you they are cold until they are shivering or crying. A bag that suits a parent at 40 degrees may be inadequate for a child in the same conditions, so build margin into your choice. A cold child is miserable, and a miserable child ends a trip.

A kid's bag also has to be forgiving. Zippers must work for small hands, the hood should not slip over the face and the bag must tolerate spills, mud and the occasional accident. These practical needs often matter more than a small difference in weight or a high fill power.

## Temperature rating: how to read it for children

Most kids' bags list a single number, such as 30 degrees or 40 degrees, often without citing the standard used. The adult ISO 23537 standard applies to adult-size bags, and many kids' bags are not tested under it, so ratings are more approximate. Treat the stated number as the Limit or even optimistic, and subtract 10 degrees when you decide. If the forecast low is 50 degrees, choose a bag rated for 40 or colder. If it is 40, choose 30. For how the ratings are defined for adult bags, see [sleeping bag temperature ratings explained](/sleep-gear/sleeping-bag-temperature-ratings-explained).

| Expected low (F) | Choose a bag rated | Add |
|---|---|---|
| 60 and above | 50 to 60 | Light blanket |
| 50 to 59 | 40 to 50 | Hat for sleeping |
| 40 to 49 | 30 to 40 | Insulated pad, warm base layer |
| 30 to 39 | 20 to 30 | Hat, socks, R-value 3 or more pad |
| Below 30 | 10 or lower | Consider waiting until the child is older |

A warm base layer, wool or fleece socks and a hat add several degrees. Dress the child in dry clothing and avoid cotton, which holds moisture. Make sure the sleeping pad provides adequate insulation, as the ground steals more heat than the air.

## Length and fit

A bag that is too large leaves empty space that the child's body must heat, and it can twist around them. Choose a bag 6 to 12 inches longer than the child's height, which gives a little growth room without wasting warmth. Many kids' bags are adjustable: a drawstring or a built-in extender lets you shorten the bag for a smaller child. Some use a footbox with a zipper that expands for growth.

Girth matters too. The shoulder and hip widths should leave a little room but not so much that the child slides around. Test the bag at home by having the child lie in it with their usual pajamas and a hat, zip it up and see if they can roll over comfortably. For smaller children, the guidance on mummy vs rectangular sleeping bags applies as well.

## Shape: mummy, rectangular or something else

**Rectangular bags** give kids room to wiggle and feel less confining. They are cheaper, unzip into blankets and work well for sleepovers and warm-weather camping. They are less warm for the weight.

**Mummy bags** hold heat better and suit cold nights, though some children dislike the confinement. A semi-mummy or "kid-fit" cut with a hood is a good compromise.

**Wearable or "sleep sack" bags** with arm and leg holes suit toddlers and cold cabin nights. They are not for rolling around a tent at night but can be useful.

For most family car camping, a rectangular or semi-rectangular synthetic bag is the practical choice. For backpacking or cold nights, choose a mummy.

## Insulation: why synthetic wins for most kids

Kids' bags are usually synthetic for good reasons. Synthetic insulation keeps most of its warmth when damp, dries quickly and survives repeated washing, which is useful when a child spills a drink or has a night accident. It is also cheaper, and children outgrow bags quickly, so spending a lot on down for a growing child rarely makes sense. See [down vs synthetic sleeping bags](/sleep-gear/down-vs-synthetic-sleeping-bag) for the trade-offs.

## Features that matter for children

- **Easy zippers** with large pulls that small hands can operate, and a zipper garage or guard that stops snags.
- **Two-way zippers** that let the child get out without fully unzipping.
- **A hood that cinches** with a simple drawcord, not a complicated toggle system.
- **Soft lining**, such as brushed polyester or flannel, which feels cozy.
- **A stuff sack with a handle** that a child can carry.
- **Glow or reflective details** that help find the bag in a dark tent.
- **A pocket** for a small stuffed animal or a flashlight.
- **Machine-washable construction**, and a clear care label.

Avoid bags with long drawstrings near the face in the hood, which can be a safety concern for very young children. Check the manufacturer's guidance on age suitability, and follow safe sleep practices for infants and toddlers, who should not sleep in loose bags with hoods that can cover the face.

## Pads, pillows and the rest of the sleep system

The bag is only part of the system. A child lying on a thin foam mat on cold ground will lose heat from below, regardless of the bag. Choose a pad with an R-value matched to the season: roughly 2 for summer, 3 for shoulder season and 4 or more for near-freezing. A self-inflating pad or a closed-cell foam pad that fits the child's length works. A small pillow or a stuff sack full of clothes helps with comfort and head position.

Layer correctly. A child who is too warm will sweat, and then get cold. Dress them in a thin base layer and add or remove layers. A warm drink before bed, a snack and a trip to the bathroom prevent most midnight wake-ups. ## Toddlers and infants

Very young children are the most vulnerable to cold and overheating and cannot regulate temperature well. Choose a bag designed for toddlers, with a proper fit and rating, and keep them in a tent with an adult who can check them. Use extra care with the temperature, since small bodies cool fast. The best camping sleeping bags for toddlers list and the guide to toddler backpacking sleeping bags show how designs adjust for size. Consult your pediatrician for guidance on camping with infants, especially regarding sleep safety.

## Making a bag last as the child grows

Kids can grow several inches a year, so a bag that fits now may be too short next season. To get more use, buy a bag with some growth room or an adjustable foot. Keep the bag clean and dry, store it loose and teach the child to roll rather than stuff it. When a child outgrows a bag, pass it to a younger sibling or friend, or donate it. A bag with a flexible design lasts longer, and a quality synthetic bag can be handed down through several children.

## Age by age: what to look for

**Ages 2 to 4.** Safety and simplicity come first. Choose a bag made for toddlers with a short length, a simple zipper that does not catch skin and no loose cords. Rectangular shapes feel less trapped, and a warm blanket layered on top is easier to adjust than a bag. Keep sleeping areas close to a parent and check the child during the night. Ratings should be conservative, with a 15 degree margin because toddlers wake cold and cannot say so.

**Ages 5 to 8.** Children this age are active and love gear that feels like theirs. A bag with a favorite color or pattern increases the chance they will sleep well in it. A 6 to 8 inch length margin works, and a semi-mummy shape starts to make sense for cooler nights. Teach the child to zip and unzip the bag, and to hang it up to air in the morning. Synthetic is still the right call.

**Ages 9 to 12.** Kids this age may join backpacking trips and carry their own gear, so weight starts to matter. A lightweight synthetic mummy in a youth size, with a compression sack, works well. Some can use adult small or women's bags with good fit. Encourage them to learn their own warmth: how many layers they need, when they get cold and what helps.

**Teens.** Most teens fit adult bags. A women's or regular cut in the right length gives better warmth than a kids' cut that is too short. Teens who backpack can step up to a down bag if they can care for it, and the [best down sleeping bags for side sleepers](/sleep-gear/best-down-sleeping-bags-for-side-sleepers) list helps restless sleepers who need extra room.

## Testing a bag before the trip

A backyard campout is the best way to test a child's bag. Choose a night with a low a little below what you expect on the trip, set up a tent or even sleep on the porch, and let the child sleep in the bag with the pad and clothing you plan to use. In the morning, ask how they slept: Did they get cold, and where? Was the zipper easy? Did the hood stay in place? A single night uncovers issues that no spec sheet will.

Take notes and make adjustments: a thicker pad, a warmer hat, a liner or a different bag. If the child sleeps well at home, the odds of a good trip improve, because comfort and confidence carry over. A child who wakes up proud of sleeping outside is more likely to want to do it again, which is what every camping parent is hoping for.

## Where to spend and where to save

Children's gear rewards smart priorities. Spend first on the pad, because a child's cold night almost always starts from below, and a pad with adequate R-value works for years. Spend second on a decent synthetic bag with a believable rating and a zipper that works, since a stuck zipper at 2 a.m. is a family-wide problem. Save on extras: a fancy stuff sack, a hood design with ten adjustments and a premium shell fabric are all wasted on a child who will drag the bag across a tent floor.

Used gear is a fair option for fast-growing kids. Check a secondhand bag for zipper function, clean lining and even loft, and wash it before use. Avoid anything with a musty smell that does not wash out. Hand-me-downs between siblings make the cost per child small, and a well-made synthetic bag survives several users.

## A kid's sleep kit checklist

Put together a small kit that goes with every trip, so you do not forget the items that make the night work.

![Beachcomber model child's sleeping bag; made by Arthur Ellis and Co Ltd of Dunedin, Christchurch and Invercargill](/images/info/how-to-choose-a-sleeping-bag-for-kids-3.webp)

- A bag with a margin on the rating, and a stuff sack.
- A pad with the right R-value and length, and a repair patch.
- A warm hat, dry socks and a base layer for sleeping only.
- A small pillow or a stuff sack for a pillow.
- A headlamp or night light and spare batteries.
- A comfort item such as a stuffed animal, kept dry in a plastic bag.
- A spare set of dry sleeping clothes in case of accidents.
- A water bottle and a small snack by the bed.
- A change of sheet or liner for easy washing.

Pack the kit in one labeled bag so you can grab it and go. Over time, adjust the contents as your child grows and your family learns what works. The goal is a routine that makes bedtime in a tent feel familiar and safe.

## Sleepovers, camps and school trips

Kids' bags are used in more places than tents. For backyard campouts, sleepovers and school or scout trips, a simple synthetic rectangular bag with a washable cover is ideal. Label the bag and stuff sack with the child's name, and make sure the child can roll and carry it. Send a pad or a thick blanket under the bag, since a thin floor can make the best bag feel cold. For camps in cooler weather, add a warm hat and a pair of dry socks for sleeping.

Talk with your child before a first night out. Let them practice getting in and out of the bag, zipping it and finding the flashlight. Make the experience comfortable and a little fun, with a favorite stuffed animal and a bedtime story. If a child is anxious about being cold, a small hot water bottle in a sock tucked near the feet before bed is a comforting trick, as long as it is tightly sealed and not too hot. Children who sleep well enjoy the outdoors more, and a good bag is the foundation.

## Care and cleaning

Kids' bags see spills and mud, so wash them as needed, following the care label. Most synthetic bags can go in a regular washer on a gentle cycle with mild detergent and dry on low. Spot clean small stains, and air the bag after each trip. Keep a sheet or liner inside the bag to protect it and reduce washing. Store the bag loose in a breathable sack, as described in how to store a sleeping bag.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Trusting the printed rating without a 10 degree margin.
- Buying a bag that is far too long for the child.
- Choosing down for a young child who will spill and outgrow it.
- Skipping a pad with enough insulation.
- Dressing the child in cotton, which holds moisture.
- Forgetting to test the zipper with small hands.
- Letting a child sleep with the hood over the face.

## Frequently asked questions

### What temperature rating should a kids' sleeping bag have?
Choose a rating 10 degrees Fahrenheit colder than the expected low. Kids lose heat faster than adults and ratings on kids' bags are often optimistic. Add a warm base layer, hat and a properly insulated pad.

### Should I buy a bag my child will grow into?
A little room is fine, about 6 to 12 inches of extra length, but a bag that is much too big is cold. If the bag has adjustable length or a foot extender, you can buy a bit larger. Otherwise buy a closer fit and replace it.

### Are synthetic bags better for kids?
For most children, yes. They are cheaper, dry fast, survive washing and keep much of their warmth when damp. Down is worth considering for older children who backpack and who can care for the bag.

### Is a mummy bag too confining for a child?
Some children dislike it, others like the cocoon. If your child tosses and turns, a rectangular or semi-rectangular bag may be better. Try the bag at home on a sleepover before a trip.

### How do I keep my child warm at night?
Use a properly rated bag, an insulated pad, a warm hat and dry base layers, and feed them a snack before bed. Avoid cotton and check that the child is not sweating. A warm water bottle in the foot area adds comfort.

### Can toddlers use adult sleeping bags?
Not safely, because adult bags are too large and can be hazardous. Use a bag designed for the child's size, keep the hood away from the face and follow safe-sleep guidance for young children, especially infants.
